Series: Adoption Mechanics: Why Correct Designs Die, and Where Human Attention Belongs

Agreement is the cheapest state any system can reach. Consensus is the most expensive way to get there.
A review has a human energy budget, and correct designs die where it goes. This series builds one model of a review: people with correlated signals and careers to protect, a vote taken in order, a chain of hand-offs, and veto gates guarding a status quo. People and AI agents differ in it only by a few parameters, several of them measured. The first part follows a design through a human review: first-writer-wins, trivia every pair must settle, and good designs aligned to death. The second gives the model agent values and finds machines that agree for free too, wrong on the same hard questions. The third gives AI a job, not a seat: the Residual Protocol spends machines on everything that can be written and a fixed budget of human attention on what cannot. Every number comes from executed code, and every part names the observation that would prove it wrong.

3 posts in this series

  1. 1. Correct, and Rejected

    Every engineer has watched a better design lose a review that nobody rigged. Asch showed how a room bends a judgment, cascade theory how the first voices settle a meeting, Parkinson how the bike shed gets the hour, and political science how veto holders guard the status quo. This post stacks those findings into one model of a review and finds where correct designs die: in trivia, or aligned to death before anyone says no. Better slides will not save them. Moving a gate might.

    14 November 2026

  2. 2. Smarter Agents, Fewer Opinions

    Swap the reviewers for AI agents and the office politics disappear, along with the excuses. The trouble stays: models trained on the same internet stumble on the same hard questions, and public benchmarks show the resemblance growing as the models get smarter. Credit analysts met this pattern in their best borrowers long ago. This post runs the same review with agents and finds more voices, not more opinions, and the one job people still do better: knowing what the internet never saw.

    21 November 2026

  3. 3. The Machine Gets the First Word

    Teams now bring AI into design reviews, and the reviewer's job is quietly changing from author to auditor of a machine that never tires of agreeing. This post gives AI a job, not a seat: the Residual Protocol. Machines carry everything that can be written down. People own what cannot: they answer a few routed questions alone inside a fixed attention budget, and add the ones the machine missed, because a machine's list of blind spots is always blind to its own.

    26 November 2026
