Smarter Agents, Fewer Opinions

21 November 2026 • Yuriy Polyulya
repo
#systems-thinking #game-theory #ai #multi-agent

A team that has watched a good design die in review has an obvious next move. Take the people out. Ask a panel of AI agents to review the design instead: agents have no careers to protect, no incumbent they once endorsed, no meeting to survive. They are handed the whole document every time, and they do not get tired at the end of the afternoon.

The move removes some of what Correct, and Rejected found and leaves the rest. That post built one model of a reviewer and followed a design through four stages: correlated sensing, aggregation in order, hand-off through layers and veto gates. It found that each stage charges the design something, and that one of them, a status quo inside the gates’ priorities, charges everything. None of the four stages needed a person to behave badly.

An agent panel changes the values in that model and keeps its form. The career terms go to zero. The conformity term stays, because models are trained toward agreement. The hand-off stays, because an orchestrator summarizes. The gates stay, because someone still approves the change. And the first stage, correlated sensing, does not go away at all. Of the four, it is also the stage whose agent values can be measured directly, question by question, and that measurement is the subject of this post.

Stanford’s HELM project publishes how each of dozens of language models answered each question of three standard benchmarks. Across all three the same picture appears. The odds that two models make the same mistake rise steeply with their accuracy. Most of that rise is shared difficulty: hold the question’s difficulty fixed and the odds fall back near even. The strongest models keep a residual, nearly double the odds of a shared mistake beyond what difficulty explains, and an average of their votes holds about two independent opinions.

The post asks four questions of that measurement. What does voting buy among strong models? Does the open question an earlier post left, whether agent crowds escalate through a cascade among correlated peers, have an answer in this model? When people and agents review together, when do the agents help? And can upgrading every member of a panel make the panel worse?

In brief
  • The belief. A panel of AI agents is free of the politics that stalls people, and a panel of better models is a better panel.
  • The mechanism. The same member model with agent values: no career terms, trained-in conformity, and mistakes that pile onto the same hard questions as models improve.
  • The law. Shared difficulty explains most of the dependence between models' errors; the strongest fifth keep nearly double the odds of a shared mistake beyond it, on all three benchmarks. In a large unweighted panel, agents that share a blind spot set the chance of adopting a worse design whatever the people know (Proposition 6).
  • The gap. An average of the strongest models' votes holds about two opinions, and a large majority of the strongest tier removes about a tenth of its members' error.
  • The intervention. For a question that turns on a local fact, let the people who hold it decide; add agents only once their answers carry some of that fact.
  • The bill. In the modeled panel, five partly informed people alone adopt a worse design 1% of the time; pooled without weights inside twenty blind-spotted agents, 41%.
  • Where you stand. Log your panel's answers on questions of known difficulty; dependence that survives within difficulty is a shared blind spot.

The Belief: Agents Are Free of Politics

L2 · Fit The belief has two halves. The first is that an agent review avoids the human failures. Agents do not fear for their careers or defend designs they once approved, and they do not stay silent to keep the peace. The second is that better models make better panels. If one model is right 75% of the time and a stronger one 80%, a panel of the stronger models should beat a panel of the weaker ones. Both should beat any single member.

L2 · Fit The first half is partly right, and the model says which part. The second half is the subject of a measurement and a theorem. The measurement says how much voting buys among today’s strong models, and the answer is little. The theorem says what would turn an upgrade of every member into a worse panel, and how close today’s models are to it.

The Same Member, Agent Values

L2 · Fit Definition 1 of Correct, and Rejected wrote one member with a payoff of accuracy, conformity, reputation, loss aversion and a career position. An agent fits the same definition. What changes is which terms carry weight, and for several of them the weight has been measured.

L2 · Fit The career terms vanish in their human form. An agent has no horizon over which a reputation accumulates, so the reputation term is zero. The endorsement term has an agent form, though. A system instruction that favors the current design acts like a sunk endorsement without a career behind it. So does a fine-tuned preference for familiar patterns, or a shared memory that records the incumbent’s approval. Where an agent carries none of these, the career plane that silenced the senior reviewer, and made the same reviewer the incumbent’s loudest defender, has nothing to act on.

L1 · Bound Conformity does not vanish. Sharma and coauthors showed that assistants trained on human preference judgments learn to agree with the user, and traced the tendency to preference data that rewards agreement[1] . The conformity term that a human reviewer pays in social discomfort, a model pays through its training.

L1 · Bound Its size in a panel has been measured. Hao and six coauthors ran multi-agent debates on a hard benchmark and separated two causes of an agent changing its answer[2] . In their primary setting, 37% of agent-question observations changed under self-reflection alone, with no peers present: that is instability, the term . Strict conformity to peers came to about 29%, and it was mostly harmful, turning correct answers into wrong ones.

L2 · Fit The human comparison is close. Franzen and Mader’s replication of Asch, cited in the earlier post, found 33% errors under group pressure. An agent’s change of answer is not a person’s strategic choice. Part of it is sampling instability, which Hao and coauthors separate out, and the rest is a learned pull toward agreement. The model needs the size of each, not its motive, and the sizes are similar.

L1 · Bound The write-back term changes form and keeps its size. Turpin and coauthors showed that a model’s chain of thought can omit the feature that actually drove its answer, giving a plausible reason that the bias never appears in[3] . Chen and coauthors gave reasoning models hints and checked whether the models mentioned the hints they used: they mostly did not[4] . Proposition 4 of the earlier post then applies to any pipeline that learns from its agents’ logged rationales. Its second part applies too: no audit of the rationales alone can find the bias. The check has to come from telemetry of what the agents actually did.

L2 · Fit The hand-off and the gates stay as they were. An orchestrator that summarizes three agents’ findings for a fourth is a hand-off with a candor of its own. A change that an agent panel recommends still passes through human approvers, or through automated guardrails whose rules play the role of ideal points. Proposition 3 of the earlier post does not care what the gates are made of. The write-back loop stays too, in the same coupled form. Rationales logged after a decision shape the next prompts, the agents’ shared memory and the guardrails’ rules. A biased record then raises the next panel’s correlation and keeps the status quo among the gates’ priorities. These links are a qualitative coupling: their direction is clear, and none of their sizes is calibrated.

Correlation Measured

L2 · Fit The sensors are where agents differ most, and where the difference can be measured directly. A human reviewer’s errors cannot be logged question by question across thousands of decisions. A model’s can. The question is how the dependence between two models’ errors changes with how good the models are, and what it is made of. Multiple-choice benchmarks measure knowledge and bounded reasoning on closed questions, while a design review weighs trade-offs whose answers are often conditional. Treating a model’s benchmark accuracy as its precision in a review is an analogy, and the post marks the transfer as a Fit.

L1 · Bound Stanford’s HELM project evaluates models on fixed benchmarks and publishes each model’s answer to each question[5] . Three of its benchmarks are used here. MMLU, a multiple-choice test of academic knowledge, appears in HELM Lite: five subjects, 567 questions, 95 models from 20 providers[6] . GPQA, graduate-level science questions written to resist search, and MMLU-Pro, a harder ten-choice version of MMLU, appear in HELM Capabilities with 65 and 64 current models[7] [8] . For every pair of models the analysis keeps four counts per group of questions: both right, each right alone, both wrong.

L2 · Fit Two correlations come out of the counts, and they govern different things. The latent correlation treats each model’s correctness as a threshold on a normal score. It is the quantity in Proposition 1, so it sets the error floor of a majority vote. The correlation of the right-or-wrong answers themselves, , is smaller. It sets the variance of an average of votes, and is the most independent opinions such an average can carry as the panel grows. That count describes the variance of a linear average of votes, not the information the votes hold, and the floor of a majority comes from the latent model instead. It is also bounded by the two models’ accuracies. Two models with very different error rates cannot have an near one, even if every error of the better one is also an error of the worse.

L1 · Bound A pooled correlation mixes two things. Two models that fail independently on any single question will still look dependent across a set of questions, if both pass the easy ones and both miss the hard ones. Psychometrics separates the two by conditioning on item difficulty. Here each pair’s questions are split into ten groups of equal size by how many of the other models answered them correctly. The pair’s own answers therefore do not define its groups. Within each group the dependence is measured as an odds ratio of a shared mistake. That is one model’s odds of a mistake when the other errs, divided by its odds when the other is right; 1 means no dependence. The groups are combined with the estimator of Mantel and Haenszel, which stays finite in the small groups where a correlation per group would not[9] .

L3 · Estimate Pooled over all questions, dependence rises with capability on every benchmark. On MMLU, the odds ratio of a shared mistake is 1.8 for the weakest fifth of pairs and 13 for the strongest; on GPQA and MMLU-Pro the strongest fifth reach 9 and 19. However large the panel, an average of the strongest fifth’s votes can hold at most 1.96 independent opinions on MMLU, 2.23 on GPQA and 1.93 on MMLU-Pro.

L3 · Estimate Within groups of similar difficulty, most of that disappears. For the middle three fifths of pairs, the within-difficulty odds ratio sits between 0.82 and 1.09 on all three benchmarks. The strongest fifth keep more: 1.25 on MMLU, 1.81 on GPQA and 1.84 on MMLU-Pro.

L3 · Estimate An odds ratio near 1 is not automatically the absence of dependence, because the estimator has artifacts of its own. Ten groups of equal size leave some difficulty inside each, and most in the hardest group. That is where the strongest models make most of their errors, and where the estimator puts most of their weight. Comparing the observed shared errors with a null separates real excess from those artifacts. The same data were simulated from an item-response model in which a model’s chance of a right answer depends only on its ability and the question’s difficulty. In it, models share nothing but difficulty. On those data the same estimator reports about 1.15 for every fifth of pairs. On MMLU-Pro it reports about 1.2 to 1.4, because there the strongest pairs’ weight sits most heavily in the hardest group.

L3 · Estimate Against that null the results sharpen. The strongest fifth sit above it on all three benchmarks, clearly on GPQA (1.81 against a null of 1.10 to 1.21) and MMLU-Pro (1.84 against 1.26 to 1.49), and only just on MMLU. The middle fifths sit at or below it: mid-range models share less than a difficulty-only model predicts.

L3 · Estimate A residual among the strongest could also be label noise. A question whose official answer is wrong makes every strong model fail together, and such questions sit among the hardest. Dropping the hardest fifth of questions leaves the strongest fifth’s ratio at 1.30, 1.86 and 2.43, while the null falls to about 1.12 to 1.21. The artifact would shrink as hard questions go, and the observed residual grows. The shared mistakes that survive fall on questions most other models answer correctly, which is what a shared blind spot looks like and what a mislabeled hard question does not.

L2 · Fit The weakest fifth show a smaller residual of their own on GPQA and MMLU-Pro, 1.36 and 1.16. Shared answering habits, such as a preferred option letter, could produce it, and the counts cannot say which.

L2 · Fit Both results matter, for different questions. For a panel that faces a stream of questions, some easy and some hard, the pooled dependence sets its floor. In the model, the floor is the share of questions on which a typical member’s chance of error exceeds one half. On those a large majority locks onto a wrong answer, and no vote rescues them. For the question of whether models share blind spots beyond difficulty, the within-difficulty ratio, read against its null, answers. For most pairs they do not. For the strongest they do.

L2 · Fit The same measurement tests an assumption of the earlier post. Definition 1 gave reviewers’ errors one shared factor and nothing else; in that model, how often the other models fail a question estimates the factor, and errors are independent once it is fixed. For the middle of the range the data are consistent with that, even below the null. For the strongest pairs they are not: those pairs share a second factor, a common blind spot that difficulty does not capture. An item-response model with a discrimination per question would refine the null, and a residual that survives this one on three benchmarks is unlikely to vanish.

L1 · Bound The direction was reported before, in other measures. Kim and coauthors studied errors across many models and providers and found that models agree on about 60% of the questions where both are wrong, with more accurate models more correlated even across architectures and providers[10] . Goel and coauthors built a similarity measure that adjusts for accuracy and found that model mistakes grow more similar as capability rises[11] . Neither separates difficulty question by question. Hossain, Yousefi and Lim measured the same pattern among judges of preference tasks: ten open models carried the information of about 3.5 independent judges, two runs of one model with different seeds correlated at 0.36 to 0.39, and frontier judges from different providers correlated at 0.42, the most of any pairs[12] . They also conditioned on item difficulty, measured from the judges’ own votes, and found that shared difficulty explains part, but not all, of the dependence.

L2 · Fit The measurement here reaches the same partial answer by a stricter route. Each pair’s difficulty groups are set by the other models, so the pair’s own answers do not define them. The result is read against an item-response null in which models share nothing but difficulty. And the residual is tested by removing the hardest questions, where mislabeled answers would sit. It locates the similarity: mostly in which questions are hard, and among the strongest models, partly in shared blind spots that grow when the hardest questions go.

L1 · Bound Panels of frontier judges show the consequence. Kohli tested nine frontier models from seven families as judges on three inference datasets. The nine provided “only about 2 independent votes’ worth of information”, and the best single judge matched or beat the full panel[13] . That is a different panel, task and estimator, and it lands where the vote correlation here lands on all three benchmarks: about two opinions.

What Voting Buys Among Strong Models

L2 · Fit The measured models can be arranged as panels. The floor needs members wrong less than half the time. The analysis therefore keeps the 84 MMLU models above 50% accuracy and splits them by accuracy into four tiers of 21. Keeping only those models truncates the joint spread of capability and agreement. Each model is also scored from a single run, which cannot separate a model’s fixed blind spots from the variation between runs. Each tier is a panel: its members’ mean error, and the mean latent correlation over the pairs inside it.

L3 · Estimate A large majority of the strongest tier errs at 22%, against its members’ 25%: voting removes about a tenth of the error. An average of that tier’s votes can hold at most 1.83 independent opinions, the limit as the panel grows. That count describes how soon averaging the tier’s votes stops reducing their spread, and it leaves the error of the tier’s majority to the floor, 22%.

L1 · Bound One caution applies to the questions themselves. MMLU offers four choices, and the floor counts a panel wrong whenever most members are wrong, in any way. A plurality vote can still pick the right answer when the wrong answers split, so for multiple-choice questions the floor is an upper bound on a plurality vote’s error. The bound is exact when the errors pile onto one tempting wrong answer, and when the decision has only two options. A design review’s adopt-or-reject is the second case, so there the floor is the floor.

L1 · Bound Other measurements agree on the scale. Kim audited majority voting over 30 models on MMLU-Pro and found that simple voting beat the strongest member in only 9.98% of panels of three[14] . The floor of Proposition 1 says why: no number of correlated members passes it.

L1 · Bound Credit risk met the same pattern twenty years earlier. Lopez estimated asset correlations across firms and found them higher for firms with lower default probabilities: the better the borrower, the more its fate moves with the economy[15] . The finding went into how bank capital rules set correlation. A good borrower defaults mainly in a recession, and a strong model errs mainly on a hard question. In both, quality concentrates what is left of the risk on the common factor.

L2 · Fit This bears on the remedy The Familiarity Bias chose. Its Definition 2 draws a panel’s variants “from multiple model families whenever more than one is deployed”, because personas of one model share its weights. The tiers here mix providers, and their dependence is still high. Crossing families removes dependence that comes from shared weights. It does not change which questions are hard, and among the strongest models it does not remove the blind spots they share.

The Open Question: Cascades Among Correlated Peers

L1 · Bound Optimization and the Ceiling No Retry Can Raise left a question open. It described networks of agents converging together on escalation. Each was “reading confidence off the others rather than off its own independent signal, plausibly an information cascade or Bayesian herding effect”, and the series “has no formal result pinning down which mechanism is doing the work there”. The model of this series can say what a cascade among correlated peers does, and how it compares with no cascade at all. Whether that is what drove the escalation it described is a separate question, which this post leaves open.

L2 · Fit Proposition 2 of the earlier post assumed independent signals. With correlated signals, each member still treats the others’ signals as independent, as the classic rule does, while the signals actually share a factor. The fair comparison is a panel of the same members voting at once, blind, so that nobody sees anyone else’s answer.

L3 · Estimate With signals right 70% of the time and independent, a sequence of thirty ends in a wrong cascade 16% of the time. A blind majority of the same thirty is wrong 2% of the time. Herding costs a great deal. At a latent correlation of 0.75, the pooled level measured among the strongest models (their right-or-wrong correlation is about 0.5), the sequence and the blind majority are both wrong 28% of the time. The large-panel floor is 27%.

L3 · Estimate The tie does not depend on the classic rule, which real agents need not follow. Members who put a weight on informing those after them herd less, and among independent signals a strong weight cuts wrong cascades from 16% to 3%. At that latent correlation every rule from myopic to strongly probing lands within two points of the blind vote.

L3 · Estimate The tie is a cancellation, not an absence of effect. Sort the trials by how far the shared factor misleads. On questions that are hard but not misleading, where most members are still right, the sequence errs 26% of the time and the blind vote 15%: herding still costs accuracy there. On misleading questions, where most members are wrong, the blind vote errs 88% of the time and the sequence 72%, because an early run of right signals sometimes locks the sequence onto the truth. Those two slices hold about a quarter of the questions between them. On the deeply misleading sixth both are almost always wrong, and on the easy majority the blind vote almost never errs while the sequence errs 1% of the time, from early wrong runs. Weighted by how often each slice occurs, the costs and the luck offset to within a fraction of a point. Herding at high correlation trades accuracy on hard and easy questions for luck on misleading ones.

L1 · Bound Field data from people point the same way. Chen, Zheng and Zhu read 198,275 voting rationales from Wikipedia’s administrator elections. Later voters gave thinner, more deferential reasons, and the evidence already posted explained it better than arrival order did; yet the elections those votes shaped did not pick worse administrators[16] . A sequence can drain a vote of independent information without changing the verdict, which is what the model predicts when the voters already agree for shared reasons.

L2 · Fit So the model gives a sharper answer to the open question than the question asked for. When agents’ signals are nearly independent, herding is what makes a crowd wrong, and an independent first round is the remedy. When their signals are as dependent as strong models’ errors are across a mix of questions, the crowd is wrong together whether or not it herds. No change to how it votes helps much then. Which mechanism drove a particular escalation then matters less than it seemed. A shared hard case produces the same outcome either way, and only a member who finds that case easy changes it.

Mixed Panels: The Informed Minority, Diluted

L2 · Fit A mixed panel of people and agents is the configuration most organizations are moving to. Agents bring breadth and cheap opinions that share blind spots. People bring local knowledge, the facts about this system, this team and this incident that no training corpus held. A local fact is something like a storage tier that loses acknowledged writes when one region fails over before another, recorded in one postmortem and nowhere else. A general trade-off is whether a cache should write through or write back for a given mix of reads and writes, which the agents’ training covers well. The question is how to combine them, and when the agents help.

L1 · Bound Prelec, Seung and McCoy proposed a rule for crowds with an informed minority[17] . Each member gives an answer and predicts how the others will answer, and the rule picks the answer that is more popular than predicted. When most members share a misconception, they also predict that most will share it. The minority who know better answer against it, and their answer turns out more common than the crowd expected.

L1 · Bound The rule fails in crowds made only of models. Denisov-Blanch and six coauthors tested crowd-wisdom strategies on language models and found that consensus, including surprisingly-popular aggregation, does not verify truthfulness when the models share their errors[18] . The rule needs a minority that holds private knowledge the majority lacks. A crowd of models trained on overlapping data has no such minority.

L2 · Fit A mixed panel can supply the minority, and the test runs on both states of the world, as Definition 3 of the earlier post requires. The model takes twenty agents and five people. Two in five of the people hold the local fact and answer it; the rest favor the successor one time in five. The agents favor the successor 30% of the time when they cannot see the fact, correlated at 0.3 or 0.6, and a knob gives them some of it. At the knob’s zero the agents answer the same way in both worlds; above zero, their chance of favoring the successor differs between the worlds by the knob’s value.

L3 · Estimate With blind-spotted agents correlated at 0.6, the surprisingly-popular rule, each member predicting the crowd from their own answer as the rule specifies, adopts the successor 55% of the time when the local fact favors it and 41% when the fact opposes it. A plain threshold at the share an uninformed crowd would give does about the same, 50% and 40%. The five people deciding alone adopt 53% of the time when the fact favors the successor and 1% when it opposes it. The people carry all the information about the local fact, and the panel dilutes it.

L2 · Fit The structural reason is unweighted pooling under correlated errors. The twenty agents carry no information about the local fact, yet their answers swing together with their shared factor, and those swings fill most of the panel. Even when a minority of people holds the local fact, an unweighted rule dilutes it whenever the agents’ common swing is large enough to move the tally or the prediction. Each agent’s own answer moves its prediction of the others, but less than the panel’s share actually moves, so the surprisingly-popular rule still fires on a favorable swing. Weighting fixes this by construction. A rule that discounts members by their measured reliability returns the people’s discrimination, and so does one that conditions on where a claim comes from or routes the local question to the people who hold the fact. The result is about unweighted pooling, not about dividing work between them, and for a large panel it can be stated exactly.

L2 · Fit The swing can be made exact. Let the panel grow while keeping its proportions, so that the agents’ share of answers settles on what their shared factor dictates and the people’s share settles on its average. What is left to chance is the shared factor alone.

L2 · Fit The proposition locates the failure precisely. The rule is set where nothing is happening: it adopts when the panel looks more favorable than it would if the successor were worse. When the successor is worse, the people answer exactly as that expectation assumed, and the panel sits on the threshold’s edge. What decides is the agents’ shared mood on the day, a coin tilted only slightly toward the status quo. No number of informed people tilts it further, because informed people change their answers only when the successor is better. Knowledge can lift a better design; only independence protects against a worse one. The gap the people can open shrinks as the agents’ correlation grows, which is why the remedy is weighting by measured reliability and not more people.

Physical translation. Set an alarm to sound when a room is louder than a quiet room usually is, then put twenty radios in it tuned to one station. The alarm now sounds whenever the station plays something loud. The five people who saw the fire can make it sound a little more often when there is one, but nothing they know stops the radios from setting it off when there is none.

L3 · Estimate The machines also spend energy differently. An agent’s opinion costs the agent nothing, and the people pay for it. Put twenty agents into the first post’s meeting as voters, holding opinions only as often as the people do, and every pair of holders still has to be reconciled. The expected settlements for a design reviewed whole rise from about 4 to about 24, and for each piece of a design in four pieces from 34 to 190. Proposition 6 says what those settlements buy against a worse design: nothing. Machine opinions are free to produce and expensive to read.

L3 · Estimate Agents start to help once their answers carry some of the fact. Pool the whole panel at the midpoint between the two worlds’ expected shares, and compare its gap with the people’s own, 0.52. The panel overtakes the people once the agents’ answers differ between the worlds by 20 to 30 points, at a correlation of 0.3. At a correlation of 0.6 they must differ by 30 to 40 points. More dependent agents need more information before they add anything.

L2 · Fit The division of labor this suggests is less flattering to aggregation than the rule’s promise, and more useful. For a question that turns on a local fact, the people who hold it are the panel until the agents can be shown to carry part of it. That can be measured: run the agents on past local questions whose answers are known, and compare their answers across the two kinds. Agents whose answers do not move with the fact are a blind spot, however many there are.

When an Upgrade Makes the Panel Worse

L2 · Fit The measured dependence rises with capability, so an upgrade does two things to a panel at once. Each member errs less, which lowers the floor. The members’ remaining errors crowd onto the same questions, which raises it. Which effect wins is a question Proposition 1 answers exactly.

Physical translation. A crew of lookouts who each miss one ship in three, posted on different headlands, almost never all miss the same ship. Between the two strongest tiers, error falls by 22% while the pooled correlation barely moves: the elasticity is 0.02 against a critical value of 2.81. Every measured upgrade improves the panel. The corollary says the room shrinks as members improve, and one bound qualifies the warning. Correlation cannot exceed 1, so along any path where error falls toward zero the realized elasticity must fall toward zero too, and the floor can never exceed a member’s own error. The danger is a path on which correlation keeps climbing while it still has room, faster than the shrinking critical value allows. The corollary also assumes one shared factor. Among the strongest pairs, which share a second one, it is an idealized reading rather than an exact law.

L2 · Fit The condition is not about models. It applies to any team whose members improve together. A company that raises its hiring bar by recruiting from the same three programs is moving along an upgrade path. Each hire is stronger, and the team’s hard cases become the same hard cases. Hong and Page showed in a model of problem solvers that a group of diverse, less able agents can outperform a group of the most able ones, because the most able tend to be alike[19] . Proposition 5 and its corollary state the trade in the unit a voting panel uses, with an exact boundary that tightens as the members get better.

Scaling the Panel, the Chain and the Gates

L1 · Bound Each stage of the pipeline scales differently as an agent system grows. Panel size is the simplest. Adding agents is cheap, and the earlier post’s bound says why it rarely helps. No equal-weight average of votes carries more than independent opinions, about two for strong models, and no majority errs less than the floor. One Equation Governs CPU Caches, Human Teams, and AI Agent Systems found a ceiling on team size from coordination cost, , past which throughput falls. The correlation ceiling is a second ceiling, on information rather than throughput, and a panel of strong models gets most of it from its first few members.

L1 · Bound Chain depth scales worst. Each hand-off keeps a share of the signal’s amplitude and of its signal-to-noise ratio, so a chain of agents that each summarize for the next keeps . Agent pipelines are often deeper than human reporting chains, because adding a summarizing step costs one call. Six summarizers at a candor of 0.85 keep about a seventh of the signal-to-noise ratio the first stage had.

L1 · Bound Gates scale toward the cliff. The convex hull of a set of ideal points contains the hull of every subset, so adding a gate can only enlarge it. By Proposition 3 of the earlier post, an added gate can close an open win-set and never opens a closed one. Automated guardrails are cheap to add, and each one is a gate with an ideal point. A system that adds a guardrail after every incident is moving its status quo toward the inside of the hull, one rule at a time.

Levers, for Agents

L2 · Fit Each lever of the earlier post switches off one force, and each has an agent form. A blind poll for agents is an independent first round: every agent answers before seeing any other’s output. The champion lever changes its mechanism in translation. The earlier post insured a person whose career would otherwise keep them silent, so insurance changed whether a well-founded view was heard. An agent has no career to insure. Its nearest form is a designated agent instructed to argue the minority case, with that role fixed in advance, and that injects an argument whether or not the evidence behind it is sound. It still carries the champion’s cost. In the earlier post a champion speaking first raised adoption of a worse design from 20% to 38%, so an advocate agent needs a verification step that can reject what it argues. A decorrelated panel is a panel whose members fail on different questions, which for strong models usually means people with local knowledge. A reversible pilot is the same for any reviewer. Stated reasons checked against telemetry are the agent form of checking a postmortem, and Proposition 4 says nothing less will do.

L1 · Bound Definition 2 of Correct, and Rejected gives one sequencing rule exactly. While the status quo sits inside the gates’ hull, the spatial test is zero, and every lever on sensing, aggregation or hand-off is worth nothing. That holds for an agent pipeline whose gates are guardrails as much as for a committee. Read the gates first.

L3 · Estimate Past the gates, the earlier post’s region decides, and it transfers to agents directly. The widest gap there needs a decorrelated panel in every setting tried, and alone it is the lever that widens the gap between adopting better and worse designs most, from 0.52 to 0.58. A blind first round alone makes a review cautious and narrows the gap. A designated advocate alone narrows it too, because an advocate for a worse design argues just as well. Together the two narrow it 0.08 less than their separate effects would.

L2 · Fit The reading for an agent pipeline follows. Check the gates first, because nothing else matters while they are closed. Then change who is on the panel, because the widest gap never forms without members who fail on different questions. Make the first round blind before appointing any designated advocate, so that the advocate’s voice is one input rather than the start of a cascade.

Reading Your Own Panel

L2 · Fit For an agent panel, the first reading is the one the agents make easy. Take a set of questions whose answers are known, ideally from the panel’s own domain, and log each agent’s right or wrong answer. For each pair of agents, count the four outcomes. The reciprocal of the mean vote correlation is the most independent opinions an average of their votes can hold.

L2 · Fit Then split the questions into groups by how hard they were for the other agents, and measure the dependence again within each group, pooled with the Mantel-Haenszel estimator. Dependence that survives inside the groups is a shared blind spot, and adding a model from different data may help with it. Dependence that vanishes inside the groups is shared difficulty, and only a member who finds those questions easy will help, which for local questions usually means a person.

L2 · Fit Before an upgrade, compute the margin. Measure the current members’ error and correlation, and the candidates’. A positive margin means the upgraded panel will do better on the panel’s own questions. A negative one means the upgrade is buying member accuracy with panel accuracy, and the panel would be better served by keeping a weaker but different member. Near the top of the accuracy range, check the elasticity against the corollary: the room shrinks there.

L2 · Fit For a sequential agent pipeline, check whether later agents see earlier agents’ outputs before they answer. If the agents’ signals are nearly independent, that sequence costs accuracy and a blind first round recovers it. If they are strongly dependent, the sequence costs little and the blind round recovers little. For a mixed panel, find who holds the local fact, let them decide on it, and measure whether the agents’ answers move with the fact before adding them.

Where This Sits Among Known Results

L1 · Bound Ensemble learning has studied diversity for decades. Kuncheva and Whitaker compared ten measures of diversity among classifiers and found that their relationship to ensemble accuracy is weak and depends on the members’ accuracy[20] . That literature knows that accuracy and diversity trade against each other, and recent work on model panels has measured the trade among language models.

L2 · Fit This post adds four things. The measurement separates question difficulty from shared blind spots, pair by pair, on three benchmarks, against an item-response null, and finds a residual blind spot at the top of the capability range that survives removing the hardest questions. Hossain and coauthors found that difficulty explains only part of judges’ dependence; the null and the survival test are what this measurement adds. Proposition 5 and its corollary give an exact boundary for the large-panel case, and show that it tightens as members improve. Proposition 6 makes the dilution of an informed minority exact for a large panel. Adoption of a worse design is set by the agents’ shared factor alone, and the gap the people can open shrinks as that factor’s correlation grows. And the mixed-panel analysis turns the informed-minority idea into a measurable condition: how much of the local fact the agents must carry before they help. The search for prior statements of the boundary covered ensemble learning, forecast aggregation, credit risk and recent work on model panels. It found the trade-off described in each, and not the condition along an upgrade path.

L2 · Fit Sorted by origin, the results of this post fall into three groups. Proposition 5, Corollary 1, Proposition 6 and Definition 4 are new as stated, and rest on Proposition 1 of the earlier post, itself Vasicek’s limit. Proposition 6 applies that limit’s conditioning argument to a panel of agents and people under a threshold rule. The rise of pooled dependence with capability agrees with the measurements of Kim, Goel, Kohli and Hossain and their coauthors. Its decomposition against an item-response null, with the residual’s survival test, is this post’s measurement; Hossain and coauthors reached the same partial answer with a different measure of difficulty. The cascades among correlated peers, the scaling of each stage and the mixed panels are estimates in this series’ model. They are built on the rules of Bikhchandani, Hirshleifer and Welch, of Tsebelis, and of Prelec, Seung and McCoy.

Falsification Criteria

Each criterion names an observation that would refute this post, with the prediction the model makes for it.

F10 (stronger models, fewer opinions).

  • Condition: per-question results of many models on a new benchmark with known answers.
  • Prediction: pooled over questions, the odds of a shared mistake rise with the pair’s accuracy, an average of the strongest models’ votes can hold fewer than 2.5 independent opinions, and within groups of similar difficulty the odds ratio sits at an item-response null except among the strongest pairs, where it stays above it.
  • Refuted if: the pooled dependence falls with capability on new data, or the within-difficulty residual among the strongest pairs vanishes on a benchmark where the middle of the range shows none.
  • Ground truth and null: the answer keys of a held-out benchmark, or the verified outcomes of real decisions; the null is the item-response model with one difficulty per question, under which models share nothing but difficulty.

F11 (the upgrade condition).

  • Condition: any upgrade of every member of a panel, with members’ error and latent correlation measured before and after.
  • Prediction: a large panel’s error falls if and only if the margin of Proposition 5 is positive, and near the top of the accuracy range smaller rises in correlation suffice to make it negative.
  • Refuted if: the panel improves on a path with a negative margin, or worsens on one with a positive margin, beyond sampling error.
  • Ground truth and null: a held-out evaluation set scored before and after the upgrade; the null is the floor computed as if the members’ errors were independent.

F12 (correlation erases the cost of herding).

  • Condition: the same members deciding in sequence and in a blind vote, with independent and with correlated signals.
  • Prediction: the sequence’s excess error over the blind vote shrinks as signals correlate; in the model it falls from 14 points with independent signals to nothing at a latent correlation of 0.75.
  • Refuted if: the sequence’s excess error is as large under correlated signals as under independent ones.
  • Ground truth and null: the answer keys of the questions; the null is the same agents answering blind.

F13 (the informed minority, diluted).

  • Condition: mixed panels of agents and people of whom some hold a local fact, run on questions where the fact favors the successor and where it opposes it, with the agents’ sensitivity to the fact measured on past questions.
  • Prediction: agents whose answers do not move with the fact dilute the people’s information under any rule tried; agents whose answers move with it by enough improve on the people alone, and more dependent agents need to move more. In large panels pooled without weights, adoption of a worse design does not fall as more of the people hold the fact.
  • Refuted if: blind-spotted agents improve on the people alone, informative agents fail to once they move with the fact by more than the predicted amount, or adoption of a worse design in a large unweighted panel falls as more of the people hold the fact.
  • Ground truth and null: the local fact verified after the decision; the null is the people’s own majority.

The Review, Replayed

The design review of Correct, and Rejected took ninety minutes and ended with a decision to revisit next quarter. Run it again with everything the series has found attached to it.

The fifteen reviewers came from teams paged by the same incidents, so their errors correlated, and an average of their votes counted as about five independent votes, each carrying less than a score. The first two to speak were not the champion, and the vote went in order, so the meeting settled on its answer after a few voices. The senior architect who had approved the incumbent five years earlier said nothing in the successor’s favor. The same architect spoke warmly of the incumbent’s record, because the same endorsement that made the first costly made the second cheap. The verdict went to the gates through three summaries and arrived with about 38% of its signal-to-noise ratio.

None of that decided the outcome. The incumbent had been negotiated for years among the architecture board, the engineering manager, security and product, until it sat inside their priorities. Every direction the successor moved cost one of them something. The adoption probability was zero before the meeting began, and the meeting could only choose the words of its decision.

Replace the fifteen reviewers with fifteen strong agents and the architect’s silence goes away, along with the defence. The agents miss the same hard questions, and the strongest of them share blind spots besides; an average of their votes carries about two opinions. Whether they vote in order or blind barely matters, because on the hard question they are wrong together either way. The summaries are cheaper, so there are more of them. The gates are unchanged, and the adoption probability is still zero.

What would have changed the outcome is the same for both panels. Someone had to look at the gates first, see that the status quo sat inside them, and change a gate. The switch could be made reversible, one exterior veto removed, or one gate’s priorities rewritten so the hull no longer contained the incumbent. After that, a panel whose members fail on different questions and a blind first round would have done their work. The two engineers who knew the local failure modes would have decided the question only they could answer.

What This Post Did Not Claim

Reversal condition. The advice of this post is to measure a panel’s dependence, pooled and within difficulty, before trusting its votes, and to let the people who hold local facts decide on them. It reverses for questions whose answers are well covered by shared training data, where the strongest single model is already the best panel and people add only noise.

What Is Not Claimed

The measurements come from three benchmarks and one run per model. A benchmark question is not a design review, and the transfer of the measured dependence to review decisions is a Fit. The paradox of Proposition 5 is proven as a condition and shown on a synthetic path. The measured models do not show it, and the post does not claim they do.

The shared factor is normal, and a normal factor has no tail dependence: it lets errors coincide on hard questions only as often as their overall correlation implies. If models fail together on the hardest questions more often than that, the true floor is higher than the one computed here, and the residual dependence the strongest pairs keep within difficulty points in that direction.

The floor scores answers as right or wrong, so on multiple-choice questions it bounds a plurality vote’s error from above rather than predicting it. For a two-option decision such as adopting a design, it is exact. Nor is it claimed that the cascade model explains any measured escalation among agents. It shows what correlated peers in sequence do, under rules from myopic to strongly probing, and that at high dependence the sequence barely matters.

Three open questions are named on purpose. The null used here gives each question one difficulty. Questions also differ in how sharply they separate strong models from weak ones, and a null with a discrimination per question could absorb part of the strongest pairs’ residual. Fitting it needs per-question records, which this analysis does not store. The mixed-panel results use illustrative constants, and no measured panel of people and agents has been run. And the instability of agents, 37% in the cited study, enters the model only as a parameter; how it interacts with cascades is not derived. Three further gaps are named as well. Proposition 5 and Corollary 1 assume one shared factor, while the measurement found a second among the strongest models. An upgrade that lowers average error while raising correlation on unusual cases is outside them. The null has no guessing floor for multiple-choice questions. And no controlled study of mixed panels of people and agents on engineering decisions, with outcomes observed after deployment, has been run.

Where Each Claim Holds

The claims of this post leave their conditions in six recurring ways, and each is listed in full in the drawer below with its assumption and failure mode.

Tasks unlike the benchmarks. Questions with local, unpublished answers may show models sharing more than difficulty, because no model’s training covered them. Open-ended judgments may show more or less dependence than multiple-choice ones.

Small panels. Proposition 5 is about the large-panel floor. A panel of three or five can behave differently, and the measured gain from voting at those sizes is smaller still.

Agents with independent signals. The finding that herding barely matters needs strongly dependent signals. Agents that fail on different questions herd in the classic way, and for them a blind first round recovers most of the loss.

Agents that gather evidence. Agents that run tests, read logs or exchange checkable proofs change their own precision and independence as they work. The measured dependence describes agents that answer from what they already know.

Weighted or gated panels. A panel can weigh members by measured reliability, or route a local question to those who hold the fact. Either avoids the dilution of the mixed-panel result by design.

People who are not informed. The mixed-panel results come from people who hold the local fact. People who share the agents’ training, or who defer to the agents’ answers, hold nothing for the panel to dilute.

Claim. Agents have no career terms, and conformity and unfaithful reasons remain at measured sizes.

  • Assumption: the cited measurements transfer to agents reviewing designs.
  • Failure mode: agents given persistent identities and records, which restore a reputation term.

  • Failure mode: other benchmarks, open-ended tasks, sampling at other temperatures, or difficulty left inside the groups.

  • Assumption: the reciprocal of the mean vote correlation, an equal-weight average.
  • Failure mode: weighted averages, or panels whose members differ widely in accuracy.

Claim. Voting removes about a tenth of the strongest tier’s error.

  • Assumption: the tier treated as a large panel under Proposition 1; right-or-wrong scoring.
  • Failure mode: plurality votes over split wrong answers, which can do better; small panels.

  • Assumption: members who treat signals as independent; thirty members.
  • Failure mode: members who know the signals are correlated, or signals that are nearly independent.

Smarter Agents, Fewer Opinions
Read the Cognitive Map as plain text

Agent Values

  1. An agent has no horizon of reputation and no sunk endorsement, so the career plane has nothing to act on; conformity stays, measured at about 29% in one study of agent debate, with instability of 37% under self-reflection alone.
  2. A model’s stated rationale can omit what drove its answer, so a pipeline that learns from logged rationales inherits Proposition 4, including that no audit of the rationales alone can find the bias.

Dependence Measured

  1. On MMLU, GPQA and MMLU-Pro, the odds of a shared mistake rise with capability to 13, 9 and 19 times independence; within groups of similar difficulty they sit near 1 for most pairs, and against a null with no blind spots the strongest fifth keep a residual, clearly on GPQA and MMLU-Pro.
  2. An average of the strongest fifth’s votes can hold at most about two independent opinions on all three benchmarks, matching panels of frontier judges; a large majority of the strongest tier removes about a tenth of its members’ error.
  3. With independent signals a sequence is wrong 16% of the time and a blind vote 2%; at the measured dependence both are wrong about 28% of the time, a tie made of herding’s cost on hard questions and its luck on misleading ones.

Mixed Panels and Upgrades

  1. Five partly informed people alone adopt a worse design 1% of the time; inside twenty blind-spotted agents the surprisingly-popular rule adopts it 41% of the time, because the agents’ common swings fill most of the panel. Proposition 6 makes it exact for a large panel: the agents’ shared factor alone sets the chance of adopting a worse design. Every agent voting in the meeting also costs the people settlements, so the dilution is paid for in human energy.
  2. Agents improve on the people alone once their answers move with the local fact by 20 to 30 points at a correlation of 0.3, and by 30 to 40 at 0.6: more dependent agents need more information.
  3. Proposition 5: replacing every member improves a large panel if and only if the margin is positive; Corollary 1: correlation may rise only slower than a critical elasticity that falls toward zero as members improve. The measured tiers sit far inside it.

Levers and Bookend

  1. While the status quo is inside the hull no lever on sensing, aggregation or hand-off is worth anything; past it, the widest gap needs members who fail on different questions, and a blind first round must come before a designated advocate.
  2. Replacing human reviewers with agents removes the senior’s silence and defence, keeps the shared hard questions and adds shared blind spots, and leaves the gates unchanged; the adoption probability stays zero until a gate changes.

Compute it. For your agent panel, log right and wrong answers on fifty questions with known answers, compute each pair’s vote correlation, and take the reciprocal of the mean. That is how many opinions the panel holds. Then do it again within groups of similar difficulty, to see how many of them are blind spots and how many are just hard questions.

A panel of better models is a better panel of models. It is still one panel, it still finds the same questions hard, and it still answers to the same gates.
