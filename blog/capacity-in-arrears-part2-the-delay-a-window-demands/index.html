The Delay a Window Demands

  • The way out it pointed to. A signal that counts waiting work cannot saturate, so it sees the size of an overload at once. It is also driven by a backlog, and a backlog is a quantity that integrates past shortfalls.
  • What this post asks next. What does the provisioning delay do to a loop driven by a backlog, and what does a stabilization window, added to keep that loop calm, do to it?

A queue consumer is about the most natural thing to autoscale. Its work arrives as messages, its backlog is a number the broker already reports, and the obvious rule writes itself: one pod for every so many waiting messages. KEDA ships that rule for Kafka, for SQS, for RabbitMQ and for dozens of other sources. AWS documents the same idea for its own fleets as backlog per instance. It is one of the most common ways to scale asynchronous work.

Take such a consumer in a model. Each pod handles ten messages a second. Demand steps up to two thousand messages a second, which needs two hundred pods, and then stays there, perfectly flat, for twenty hours. The autoscaler aims for ten waiting messages per pod, the default threshold of KEDA’s Kafka scaler. A new pod takes 90 seconds to start consuming, and the Kubernetes autoscaler applies its default five-minute window to every scale-down.

Over the last hour of the run, with demand that never moves, the pod count does not settle. It spends most of the hour pinned at the configured ceiling of two thousand, ten times what the work needs. Then it falls to a handful of pods, as low as one, and climbs back. The fifth percentile of the hour is 8 pods, the ninety-fifth is 2,000, and the mean is 1,551.

The obvious suspect is the five-minute window, so remove it. The loop still swings from one pod to two thousand, with a mean of 936. Remove nothing, and instead raise the threshold until each pod’s share of the backlog is forty seconds of work. With the window, the loop still hunts, between 40 and 503 pods over the hour. Without it, the loop settles into a band from 186 to 234 pods.

So the window matters, but it is not the whole story, and removing it did not rescue the default. Something about a backlog-driven loop needs a standing quantity of waiting work to stay at rest, and the window raises how much. This post finds how much.

In brief
  • The belief. A stabilization window makes an autoscaling loop stable.
  • The mechanism. A backlog-scaled loop acts on the backlog of one dead time ago on the way up and, through the window, of one window ago on the way down.
  • The law. It rests only if every message waits a standing delay of at least : about one dead time at the default window, rising with the window, and never more than e dead times after an upward step. A closed form derived by harmonic balance gives the curve within 3.3% for windows from one dead time up to the default.
  • The bill. About ninety seconds of waiting on every message at a 90-second dead time, or a hunt that costs 17% to 99% more pods. The Kafka default supplies one second, and hunts with or without the window.

The Belief: the Window Makes the Loop Stable

L1 · Bound The Kubernetes documentation presents the stabilization window as the remedy for an unstable replica count. It names the problem first: “it is possible that the number of replicas keeps fluctuating frequently due to the dynamic nature of the metrics evaluated. This is sometimes referred to as thrashing, or flapping”[1] . It then gives the fix: “The stabilization window is used to restrict the flapping of replica count when the metrics used for scaling keep fluctuating.” With a five-minute window, the page explains, the controller “avoids having the scaling algorithm frequently remove Pods only to trigger recreating an equivalent Pod just moments later.”

L1 · Bound The default applies the window to scale-downs only. The documentation states it directly: “For scaling down the stabilization window is 300 seconds”, while “For scaling up there is no stabilization window.” KEDA, which turns a queue into a metric the Kubernetes autoscaler can read, passes the setting through untouched. Its specification says “KEDA would feed values from this section directly to the HPA’s behavior field,” and the example it prints sets stabilizationWindowSeconds: 300[2] .

The belief is reasonable, and for the loop the documentation has in mind it is correct. A noisy metric makes a ratio controller add and remove pods in quick succession. Each removal throws away a warm pod that the next sync will ask for again. A window that holds the highest recent recommendation stops that. It costs a few minutes of extra pods after every peak and buys a calmer count.

The belief carries an assumption it never states. It treats the window as a filter on noise, something that removes fluctuation without changing the loop it sits in. In a loop driven by a backlog the window is part of the dynamics. It holds pods after the work that justified them is gone, and holding pods drains the backlog the next decision will read.

How a Queue Reaches the Controller

L1 · Bound KEDA does not scale anything itself between one pod and many. It publishes each trigger as an external metric and creates a HorizontalPodAutoscaler that reads it. By default the metric has the type AverageValue, and KEDA’s specification spells out what follows: “If our metric is the queue size, the threshold is 5 messages, and the current message count in the queue is 20, HPA will scale the deployment to 20 / 5 = 4 replicas, regardless of the current replica count.”[2]

L1 · Bound The controller’s source agrees. For an external metric with a per-pod target, GetExternalPerPodMetricReplicas computes math.Ceil(float64(usage) / float64(targetUsagePerPod)), after the same tolerance check as every other metric[3] . The recommendation is the backlog divided by the threshold, rounded up. The current count enters only through the tolerance: if the backlog per current pod is within 10% of the threshold, the controller holds.

L1 · Bound That rule differs from the CPU path of One Bit per Dead Time in one decisive way. The CPU path counted ordered pods at zero usage, so it could not order the same capacity twice within a round. The backlog path has no such accounting. A pod that has been ordered but is not yet consuming does nothing to the backlog. The backlog keeps growing during the dead time, and the recommendation grows with it. The controller keeps ordering for work that the pods already on their way will eventually take.

L1 · Bound AWS documents a sizing rule for the same kind of loop. Its guide to scaling on an SQS queue defines backlog per instance and sets the target from the latency the application can accept: “take the acceptable latency value and divide it by the average time that an EC2 instance takes to process a message”[4] . With a ten-second acceptable latency and a tenth of a second per message, the target is a hundred messages per instance. This rule turns out to be the right one for choosing the threshold, for a reason the guide does not give. The next section shows why.

A Backlog Is an Integrator

Before the equations, one step worked through with numbers. Take a consumer of the size a test cluster can hold. Each pod handles ten messages a second, and demand steps from 50 to 200 messages a second, so the need goes from five pods to twenty. The dead time is 90 seconds, the window is the default five minutes, the replica ceiling is 100, and the threshold is KEDA’s default of ten messages per pod. At rest that threshold holds one second of work per pod.

L3 · Estimate In the first thirty seconds after the step the backlog reaches 4,700 messages, and the controller doubles its count at each of the two syncs, from five pods to twenty. The backlog keeps growing until the ordered pods start to consume, and at two minutes it still holds 14,850 messages. A hundred pods then clear it within a minute: 2,250 messages at 150 seconds and none at 180.

L3 · Estimate With the queue empty the recommendation falls to one pod, and the window holds the hundred pods for five more minutes anyway. When it lets go, the count drops at once to a single pod, and the backlog starts to build again from zero. The second climb begins from one pod instead of five, so it goes higher, past 24,000 messages, and from then on the loop repeats it exactly, every 510 seconds. Demand never moves after the step. The swing comes from three things the rest of this post takes apart: the backlog remembers every second of shortfall, the pods ordered against it arrive one dead time late, and the window keeps them one window too long on the way down.

L2 · Fit The reason a backlog loop needs a standing delay at all is the integrator. A backlog remembers every second in which capacity fell short and every second in which it ran over. A controller that reads the backlog through a dead time acts on a memory that is already out of date. The integrator keeps adding to the error while the correction is on its way. A larger threshold makes each pod’s share of the backlog larger, so the controller’s response to a given deviation is gentler. The standing delay is the price of that gentleness, paid by every message, all the time.

L2 · Fit The two loops in this series fail in opposite directions for one reason. The CPU loop of One Bit per Dead Time counted every pod on order at zero usage. It never ordered the same capacity twice, and it paid for that caution with a slow, fixed rate of learning when its metric saturated. The backlog loop learns the size of an overload at once, because its signal has no ceiling. It pays by ordering past the pods already on their way, since nothing in its signal says they exist. Both loops face the same dead time. One spends it waiting, and the other spends it accumulating a debt it will later overpay.

Two Branches, Two Delays

The deployed loop is not the pure-delay loop. Its way up and its way down pass through different mechanisms, and the difference is what the window adds.

L1 · Bound On the way up, a recommendation becomes capacity only after the dead time. A pod ordered now starts consuming 90 seconds later. If the recommendation dips in between, pods still starting are the first to go. The ReplicaSet controller ranks pods for deletion with unscheduled before scheduled, pending before running and not ready before ready[3] . So a pod reaches service only if it was wanted for the whole dead time without a break. The consuming count is the minimum of the recommendation over the last dead time.

L1 · Bound On the way down, the window holds the highest recommendation of the last W seconds, five minutes by default, and only then lets the count fall. A pod that is no longer wanted keeps consuming until the window has passed. The consuming count is the maximum of the recommendation over the last window, before the dead time’s minimum is applied to it.

Put together, and leaving aside the sync period, rounding and the tolerance, the loop is:

L2 · Fit While the backlog rises, the maximum does nothing, and the loop acts on the backlog of one dead time ago. While it falls, the window holds the old, larger value, and the loop acts on the backlog of one window ago. The way up has the dead time and the way down has the window. A window five minutes long on a loop whose dead time is ninety seconds makes the down branch more than three times slower than the up branch.

Physical translation. Picture a thermostat that switches the heating on a few minutes late and refuses to switch it off until the room has been warm for half an hour. In a room that loses heat slowly, nobody notices. In a draughty room the heating overshoots every time, then finally switches off, the room cools past the setpoint, and the late start overshoots again. The cure lies in the room rather than the switch: a room that holds more heat, with a reserve large enough that the two delays stop mattering. In a backlog-driven loop that reserve is the standing delay, and the window decides how large it has to be.

Definition 5 -- Hunting: a backlog-scaled loop hunts if a step in demand leaves a pod count that keeps swinging instead of settling

Definition 5 (Hunting). In the idealized loop, a loop hunts if the deviation produced by an upward step in demand, starting from rest, does not decay. In the deployed loop, measured over the last hour of a twenty-hour run, a loop hunts if its pod count swings by more than 30% of the pods needed.

The 30% threshold sits well above the ripple the 10% tolerance leaves in a settled loop, so tolerance ripple is not counted as hunting.

The Window That Cannot Matter

The scaling law says the needed delay grows with the window. It does not say whether it grows without limit. A window of an hour, or a day, would by that reading demand an ever larger standing delay. A second result caps it.

Deriving the Curve

The computed curve can be approached from both ends. For moderate windows the hunt looks like a sine, and a classical method of nonlinear control, the describing function, turns that into a closed form. For long windows the hunt becomes a plateau, and the structure of the delay equation near e dead times gives the shape of the approach.

The law turns the window into a cost. A loop that must rest has to carry a standing delay of , paid by every message as waiting time. A loop that carries less hunts, and hunting has a price of its own.

L3 · Estimate Hunting is expensive as well as noisy. The window holds every peak, so the hunting loop pays for its highest pod count for minutes at a time and its lowest only briefly. Its mean pod count runs 17% to 99% above the need. A team that cut the threshold to lower latency, and watched the loop hunt, pays for the hunt in pods. It does not get the latency either, because a hunting loop’s backlog swings with its pods.

What Is Already Known

L1 · Bound Backlog per instance is documented practice, and so is the latency rule for choosing its target[4] . The guide also notes why a raw queue length is the wrong metric: “A policy that targets only the number of messages in the queue does not account for the number of instances available. As a result, it can add more instances than needed while the queue drains.”

L1 · Bound The distinction between a backlog signal and a rate signal is also known. KEDA’s RabbitMQ scaler offers both, a queue-length mode “to keep number of messages in the queue at selected, nominal level” and a message-rate mode “to keep the consumption rate up to messages publication rate”[2] .

L1 · Bound Instability from delayed queue feedback is long established in networking. Hollot and three coauthors analysed active queue management for TCP flows as a feedback loop with delay and designed controllers for its stability[7] . Johari and Tan studied end-to-end congestion control[8] . Delay limits its stability there too. Stabilization windows against flapping are documented by the controller’s own maintainers.

L1 · Bound Both effects of this post were met before, with physical servers and a 260-second setup time. Gandhi and three coauthors scaled up on the number of requests in the system, a backlog-type signal. They report that their policy “overshoots while scaling up capacity”, and that “a doubling of request rate can lead to a tripling of number of requests at a single server”[9] . That is the ordering past pods already on their way that the walked-through sync shows. For scaling down they made the servers “very conservative in turning servers off”, a delayed turn-off that is the stabilization window’s ancestor, and it helped a controller driven by request rate. The law of this post concerns the combination they did not study: the window in front of a loop driven by the backlog itself.

Exact Results for Setup Times

L1 · Bound Queueing theory has exact results for a close relative of this loop. Gandhi, Harchol-Balter and Adan derived them for server farms[10] . Their model is a farm of k servers with Poisson arrivals, exponential job sizes and an exponential setup time. Idle servers turn off at once, and every job not being served puts one server into setup. Their on-demand policy is therefore a backlog-scaled loop with a threshold of one job per server and no window. Their first theorem covers staggered boot, with at most one server in setup at a time. Response time is then exactly the response time of the same farm without setup plus one setup time, a decomposition they call “counter-intuitive since not all jobs will experience the setup time”. Their second gives the exact state distribution when the number of servers is unlimited.

L3 · Estimate Those results give the simulation machinery of this series an exact test. The check is a request-level simulation, with three seeds of 300,000 jobs each and time measured in mean job sizes. Across six configurations, three of each model, five of the six exact values lie inside the range of the seeds: at a mean setup of 1 in the staggered model, for example, the exact 2.205 sits between 2.195 and 2.221. The sixth, at a mean setup of 5, lies 1.7% above the seeds’ mean. Long setups make each busy period long, so a run of fixed length holds fewer of them. The seeds spread by 3% and 5% at setups of 5 and 10, against about 1% at a setup of 1. Where exact theory exists, the simulator agrees with it to within that noise.

L2 · Fit The exact theory and this post agree where they overlap, and they measure different things. Their models are stationary Markov chains. With no window, the on-demand policy settles at every load below capacity, as the idealized loop of this post rests without a window. In the staggered model the setup time costs every job one mean setup on average, an exact and modest price, and it never makes the system hunt. Hunting needs what those models leave out: a window that holds capacity after the need has passed, in front of a threshold that reacts through a fixed dead time. Their conclusion for the expensive corner is one this series shares: when both the load and the setup time are high, “it pays to leave servers on”.

L1 · Bound The idealized loop also belongs to a known class of equations. A differential equation whose right-hand side depends on the maximum of the unknown function over a past interval is called a differential equation with maxima. Bainov and Hristova collect the theory of that class[11] . The book gives existence results, integral inequalities and stability conditions of the Lyapunov and Razumikhin kind. The loop of this post nests a minimum over the dead time around a maximum over the window, and the scaling law, the ceiling of e and its exactness were derived here for that nested form directly. Whether the general theory already contains them, or yields a proof of the parts left open, was not settled for this post.

L2 · Fit Four results were not found in the sources read for this post. The first is the min-max form of the deployed loop, with its different delays on the two branches. The second is the scaling law that reduces its stability to one curve in the window over the dead time, checked against the deployed controller. The third is the window-proof delay of e dead times, its exactness as the least upper bound of the curve, and the latency floor that follows. The fourth is the closed-form harmonic-balance approximation to the curve and the logarithmic approach to e. They are offered as this post’s contribution, with the same caution as before: absence from the sources read does not prove nobody has written them down.

What the Model Leaves Out

L3 · Estimate Noise widens the band without moving the boundary. Demand in the boundary runs is flat, so they were repeated with 10% demand noise, a multiplier redrawn every minute. At a standing delay of 40 seconds the band widens to 92 to 581 pods, and at 100 seconds it stays narrow, 183 to 229, so Definition 5 still separates the two sides. Polling the queue every 30 seconds instead of 15 changes little: 60 to 479 pods at 40 seconds and 184 to 207 at 100. At 30% noise, the band the cost figure draws, the noise alone swings a loop above its boundary by more than Definition 5’s threshold: 158 to 284 pods at 100 seconds. The definition cannot place a boundary there. The band still narrows sharply between 80 and 100 seconds, where the noise-free boundary lies.

L3 · Estimate Above the boundary the pods follow the noise, and the band widens with it. Below the boundary the swing is several times larger than the noise alone would cause. Polling the queue every 30 seconds instead of every 15 leaves the boundary in place. Noise neither creates the hunt nor removes it, and only decorates whichever regime the standing delay has chosen.

L3 · Estimate A consumer-group rebalance is different. When the number of consumers changes, a Kafka group pauses consumption while partitions are reassigned. With a 10-second pause after every change, the loop still swung between 158 and 299 pods at a standing delay of 100 seconds, and rested between 186 and 206 at 150. With a 30-second pause it was irregular at 120 and 150 seconds, and rested at 200. A plausible reading is that the pause must fit inside what the tolerance band can absorb, roughly a standing delay above the pause divided by the tolerance. That reading is untested beyond these runs and is not part of the law.

L2 · Fit Several things are not in the model at all. The partition cap clips a Kafka hunt at the partition count. Activation from zero, which KEDA handles with its own cooldown, is not modelled. Neither are metric caching between the broker and the controller, visibility timeouts on SQS, or messages that fail and return to the queue. Each could shift the boundary. None is likely to remove it, because the integrator and the two delays are still there.

The Binding Constraint, in Sequence

L2 · Fit The Constraint Sequence Framework orders the constraints by which binds first. For a backlog-scaled consumer the dead time binds first, because it sets the unit in which every other delay is measured. The window binds second, because it sets how many dead times of standing delay the loop needs. The threshold binds third: it is where the needed delay is paid, and its value follows from the first two. The latency a team promises is the outside constraint the other three must fit under.

L2 · Fit The common response to a hunting consumer runs the sequence backwards. It lengthens the window to stop the flapping, which raises the delay needed, which makes the hunt worse at the same threshold. The sequence says to start from the dead time and then shorten the window to the shortest one that still serves its purpose. Only then does the threshold get set, from the curve, with a margin.

Falsification Criteria

Each criterion names an observation on a real cluster that would refute this post. All need a queue consumer scaled through KEDA on its backlog, with a measured dead time and a known per-pod rate c, so that the standing delay T/c can be set directly. The cluster kit for this series provides one. Its broker generates demand that steps from 50 to 200 messages a second, and each consumer takes ten messages a second after a 90-second start delay, so 20 pods are needed under a ceiling of 100. The predictions are the deployed model’s at that size, over the last hour of a twenty-hour run.

F1 (the boundary exists).

  • Condition: the default 300-second window, demand stepped up and then held flat, standing delays of 60 and 150 seconds, below and above the predicted boundary of about 90 seconds for a 90-second dead time.
  • Prediction: the first run hunts in a regular cycle; the second settles within the tolerance band. At the kit’s size the model gives 6 to 35 pods at 60 seconds and 19 to 21 at 150 seconds.
  • Refuted if: the loop settles well below the predicted boundary, or hunts well above it.

F2 (the window raises the boundary).

  • Condition: the same consumer at a fixed standing delay between the one-minute and five-minute boundaries, for example 60 seconds at a 90-second dead time.
  • Prediction: it rests with a 60-second window, between 19 and 22 pods, and hunts with a 300-second window, between 6 and 35.
  • Refuted if: the longer window settles the loop that the shorter one could not.

F3 (removing the window does not rescue the default).

  • Condition: the Kafka default threshold of ten messages per pod, one second of standing delay for these consumers, with and without the window.
  • Prediction: both runs hunt across the full range, from one pod to the ceiling of 100.
  • Refuted if: removing the window alone brings the loop to rest.

Mechanism: a backlog integrates every shortfall; the controller reads it through the dead time on the way up and through the window on the way down, and orders for work that pods already on their way will take.

What This Post Did Not Claim

Where Each Claim Holds

Claim. The deployed backlog loop rests if and only if its standing delay is at least about .

  • Assumption: KEDA’s default AverageValue metric through the v1.34.0 controller on the default path, a fluid backlog, constant per-pod rate, and an upward step in demand.
  • Failure mode: a per-pod rate that falls with load, retries that return to the queue, or a partition cap below the need. Each changes the integrator or clips the swing.

Claim. The scaling law and the window-proof delay of e dead times are exact for the idealized loop.

  • Assumption: no sampling, rounding, tolerance, floor or ceiling, and an upward step from rest.
  • Failure mode: the deployed loop adds a sampling delay of about 10 seconds to each branch; the agreement in the boundary figures is evidence for the correction, not a proof of it.

Claim. Removing the window does not rescue a threshold far below the dead time.

  • Assumption: the controller’s sync and the metric refresh add a delay to the down branch.
  • Failure mode: none found in the runs; with no window the deployed boundary sat between 10 and 40 seconds of standing delay.

Reversal condition. The advice of this post is to treat the window as a cost and to size the threshold from the curve. It reverses when the backlog is not what the controller reads. A rate signal has no integrator in it, and a loop scaled on rate does not need a standing delay. That loop has a blindness of its own, which this post does not examine.

What Is Not Claimed

The laws in this post are exact for the idealized loop and checked, not proven, for the deployed one. The scaling of Proposition 4, the ceiling of Proposition 5 and its exactness in Proposition 5b are proofs. That the curve rises steadily between its two ends is observed, not proven. The curve itself is computed, its closed form is an approximation by harmonic balance, and its long-window form is fitted to a shape the argument only motivates. The sixteen deployed boundaries agree with the curve to 6% on average, and the four that miss by more than 10% all miss in the same direction.

It has not been claimed that stabilization windows are harmful. In front of a controller driven by a noisy rate, a window does what the documentation says, and Gandhi and coauthors found a delayed turn-off helpful for exactly that kind of controller. The claim is narrower: in front of a loop driven by a backlog, the window sets a price in waiting time that the documentation does not mention. Nor has it been claimed that KEDA’s defaults are wrong. Ten messages per pod is one second of waiting only for a consumer that handles ten messages a second. A consumer that spends ten seconds on each message carries a hundred seconds at the same threshold, and at a 90-second dead time it rests.

Three open questions are named on purpose. The limit of the curve at very long windows is argued, not proven. The second condition that consumer-group rebalances add is preliminary. And partition caps, metric caching and visibility timeouts are outside the model entirely.

Two credits belong here as well. The symmetric bound that Anti-Fragile Decision-Making at the Edge applied as a sufficient filter is also necessary for the pure-delay equation, which this post uses as its baseline. And the overshoot of a backlog-type signal during setup was observed with physical servers more than a decade before this series; this post adds its boundary, not its discovery.

What Is Left Open

A backlog sees the size of an overload, which the saturated metric of the first part could not. The price is that it remembers. Every second of shortfall stays in it until capacity removes it. A controller that reads it through two different delays needs a standing quantity of that memory just to rest.

Sizing the threshold correctly leaves one assumption standing. Every message in the backlog was waiting for a client who still wanted it. In the model, messages wait patiently for as long as it takes. Real requests time out, and real clients retry. A signal that counts only fresh demand avoids the backlog’s memory entirely. Whether it also avoids what the backlog was remembering is a question this post does not answer.

The Loop as It Runs

Compute it. Measure your consumer’s dead time, from the scaling decision to the first message a new pod consumes. Add about ten seconds for sampling, and divide your downscale window, plus the same ten seconds, by that effective dead time. Read from the curve, or compute it: up to about three dead times use the closed form of Proposition 5a, which errs high by at most 3.3%, and beyond that use , which errs low by at most 2.7%. The product is the standing delay your loop needs. Divide your threshold by your per-pod rate. If the result is smaller, the loop will hunt, and the cheapest fix is usually a shorter window.

A stabilization window holds pods after the work that justified them is gone. In a loop driven by a backlog, that is the instability, and the threshold is where it gets paid.
